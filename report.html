Predicting House Prices in Ames,Iowa

by Data Science Duo: Terry and Rithvic

2025-11-30

Research Question

To what extent do House features (e.g., living area, overall quality) versus locational features (neighbourhood) explain variation in house prices in Ames, Iowa? Our aim with this project is to determine whether house features or location-based variables are better predictors of housing prices.

About the data

Our project used three data sets which were subsets of the Ames Housing Data, which explores variables ranging from internal and external housing factors. We aim to locate variable’s which have the greatest correlation with the housing prices.

We cleaned the data using the tidyverse package and split the variables into category A being ‘Housing features’ involving variables OverallQuality, GrLivArea, YearBuilt and category B, being ‘Location Variables’, which includes the variables Neighborhood, LotFrontage and MSZoning.

To strengthen the modelling process and address distributional challenges, two new engineered variables were introduced:

log_saleprice: Natural log of SalePrice to better fit linear models

Mag_Price: Binary classification of SalePrice, labelled each house as either “High” or “Low”

We also recoded OverallQual into three descriptive quality categories (“Low”, “Med”, “High”) for clearer interpretation.

Variable Type Description
OverallQual Categorical Reclassified overall finish and quality of the home
GrLivArea Numeric Living area in square feet
YearBuilt Numeric Year the house was originally built
Neighborhood Categorical Residential neighborhood where the home is located
MSZoning Categorical Zoning classification for land use
LotFrontage Numeric Linear feet of street-connected property frontage
log_saleprice Numeric Natural log of SalePrice
Mag_Price Categorical Binary classification of SalePrice

Exploratory Analysis

To examine potential correlations between predictors and sale prices, scatter plots and box plots were constructed depending on variable type—continuous variables were plotted against log-transformed prices, whereas categorical variables used grouped boxplots.

Overall Quality exhibited one of the strongest relationships in the dataset. As the quality rating increases from 1 to 10, sale prices show a clear monotonic upward trend. This aligns with expectations—better materials, superior craftsmanship, and enhanced finishes typically yield higher market valuations.

GrLivArea also demonstrated a pronounced positive linear relationship with sale price. Larger homes command higher market prices, and this variable is known to be one of the strongest predictors in the Ames dataset more broadly. The scatter plot showed tight clustering around an upward-sloping line, suggesting robust predictive potential and minimal noise among mid-range values.

YearBuilt, however, presented a more complex pattern. When considering the entire range from late 1800s to 2000s, the relationship is nonlinear. A curved line of best fit indicates fluctuations in price for older homes; some homes built before the 1940s appear to command higher prices despite age, likely due to architectural charm or “vintage value.” But when focusing on homes built after the 1940s, a more consistent positive correlation emerges, indicating newer constructions tend to command higher prices due to modern materials and updated structural standards.

Neighborhood, plotted by descending median price, displayed strong differences in central tendency. Some neighborhoods clearly command higher baseline prices, but high variability within each group complicates predictive power. Certain neighborhoods also had very few observations, introducing sparsity issues that potentially weaken logistic modelling unless dummy variables are carefully handled.

LotFrontage showed a weak and noisy linear trend. While modest positive association exists, the spread is wide and the highest values appear to behave as outliers. The data also exhibited plateaus—points aligned horizontally—suggesting repeated house layouts or subdivisions with similar frontage measurements. This consistency reduces the variable’s ability to distinguish between high and low prices in a meaningful way.

MSZoning, analysed through boxplots, showed distinct clusters of sale prices among residential zones (e.g., RL, RM, C, etc.). Although zoning categories demonstrate differences in median price ranges, many classes overlap substantially, limiting discriminatory power compared to Group A variables.

Modelling Approach

Our dataset came with two different files train and test, so we did not have to do the split ourselves. Since the outcome variable Mag_Price is binary, logistic regression was selected as the predictive method for both Group A and Group B models.

log_model <- logistic_reg() %>% set_engine("glm")

A key preprocessing step in both models was step_dummy, which converts categorical variables into dummy (one-hot) numeric indicators. This was particularly essential for Group B, which contains Neighborhood and MSZoning, both high-cardinality factors.

rec_A <- recipe(
  Mag_Price ~ 
    GrLivArea + OverallQual + YearBuilt,
  data = train
) %>%
  step_dummy(all_nominal_predictors())

Group B additionally required step_unknown, a safeguard against new or unseen factor levels appearing in the test set that were not present in the training set. This ensures model stability and prevents errors during prediction.

rec_B <- recipe(
  Mag_Price ~ 
    Neighborhood + MSZoning + LotFrontage,
  data = train
) %>%
  step_unknown(all_nominal_predictors()) %>%
  step_dummy(all_nominal_predictors())

We then created a model workflow for both groups (group A is shown):

wf_A <- workflow() %>%
  add_model(log_model) %>%
  add_recipe(rec_A)

And fit the model:

fit_A <- wf_A %>%
  fit(data = train)

Model Evaluation

Both Group A and Group B achieved AUC scores slightly above 0.5, indicating predictive performance marginally better than random guessing. Such outcomes reflect moderate predictive usefulness, but highlight limitations in linear logistic modelling when applied to complex datasets with nonlinear interactions and sociogeographic influences.

Although the difference between AUC scores was small, Group A consistently achieved a higher AUC than Group B, suggesting housing features contain slightly stronger predictive information for binary classification of prices. However, the gap was narrow enough that we could not confidently claim housing features are definitively superior predictors—only that, numerically, they seem marginally more effective.
